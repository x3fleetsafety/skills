# Fatigue Management & Night Driving

Driver fatigue is one of the most preventable causes of CMV accidents. FMCSA HOS rules are minimum requirements — they don't guarantee safe driving. This skill covers the science + practical management.

## The science of fatigue

### Circadian rhythm

Human bodies operate on a roughly 24-hour cycle. Performance peaks at:
- **8-10 AM** (morning alertness)
- **3-5 PM** (afternoon alertness)

Performance troughs:
- **2-6 AM** (circadian low) — performance equivalent to legal drunk driving in extreme cases
- **1-3 PM** (afternoon dip)

Drivers operating during circadian lows are at significantly higher accident risk.

### Sleep debt

A driver can be "legally compliant" with HOS but still fatigued:

- Required: 10 hours off-duty per shift
- But required sleep: typically 7-9 hours of actual sleep
- Time spent off-duty NOT sleeping (eating, calling family, watching TV) doesn't count

A driver who takes 10 hours off but only sleeps 5 = sleep debt accumulating.

### Microsleeps

Brief unconscious episodes lasting 0.5-10 seconds:
- Driver doesn't realize it happened
- Eyes may stay open
- Brain fully shut down
- Vehicle continues at speed (catastrophic at highway speed)
- Common at 2-5 AM
- Common after sleep debt > 24 hours

A microsleep at 70 mph = 100 feet of unconscious driving. Often fatal.

## Recognizing fatigue

In yourself (driver):
- Yawning excessively
- Blinking frequently
- Difficulty focusing
- Eyes burning
- Drifting in lane
- Missing exits
- Forgetting last few miles
- Restless feet / fidgeting

In others (dispatcher / observer):
- Driver appears droopy-eyed
- Slow responses on dispatch calls
- Forgetting routine information
- Voice changes (slurred or muffled)
- Reports of unusual incidents (near-misses, hard braking)

## Night driving challenges

Night driving has multiple layered risks:

1. **Reduced visibility** — 50% less visual information available
2. **Glare from headlights** — slows recovery from bright lights
3. **Circadian low** — body wants to sleep
4. **Other drivers also fatigued** — more drunk/drowsy drivers on the road
5. **Less help available** — fewer first responders, fewer stations open
6. **Cumulative fatigue** — multi-day shifts compound

Best practices for night driving:
- Pre-trip: 7-9 hours of sleep BEFORE the night shift
- Strategic caffeine (limit late night, use early in shift)
- Bright lights in cab to stay alert
- Stop frequently (every 1-2 hours, walk + stretch)
- Have a co-driver if possible
- Don't push through extreme fatigue (pull over + nap if needed)

## HOS rules vs actual fatigue management

| HOS Rule | Reality |
|---|---|
| 11 hours driving allowed | After 7 hours, performance drops significantly |
| 14-hour on-duty window | Combined with fatigue, 14 hours becomes risky |
| 10-hour off-duty | Doesn't account for circadian timing |
| 30-min break | Helpful but not enough for severe fatigue |
| 60/70-hour weekly limit | Effective; chronic limit |

HOS rules establish minimum safety. Carrier should establish:

- **Maximum daily driving for night drivers** (e.g., 10 hours not 11)
- **Limited consecutive night shifts** (e.g., max 3-4 in a row)
- **Voluntary rest schedule** when feasible
- **Strategic napping authorization** during long trips

## Fatigue Risk Management System (FRMS)

A formal program some larger fleets implement:

### Components:
1. **Policy + procedures** — written
2. **Driver education** — training on recognizing + managing fatigue
3. **Schedule design** — minimize circadian risk (limit night shifts)
4. **Self-reporting culture** — drivers can call off without penalty if fatigued
5. **Drowsiness monitoring** — telematics-based detection
6. **Sleep apnea screening + treatment** — clinical layer
7. **Wellness program** — nutrition, exercise, sleep hygiene
8. **Incident analysis** — fatigue identified as contributing factor

FRMS is voluntary but increasingly required by major shippers + insurers.

## Drowsiness detection technology

Telematics + cab cameras can detect:

- **Eye closure rate (PERCLOS)** — % of time eyes closed; > 13% indicates drowsiness
- **Head pose deviation** — head down, drifting
- **Lane drift** — vehicle drifting toward lane edges
- **Brake/acceleration patterns** — micro-corrections
- **Reaction time** — slow response to brake light ahead

When detected, system can:
- Alert driver (audible, visual, haptic)
- Alert dispatcher
- Log for safety manager review
- Suggest rest stop

Major systems: Smart Drive, Lytx, Samsara, Geotab, Motive — all have driver-distraction + drowsiness features.

## Sleep apnea + fatigue

Obstructive sleep apnea is the #1 cause of chronic driver fatigue:
- Driver may sleep 8 hours but get poor-quality sleep
- Wakes feeling tired
- Performance impacted throughout shift

Screening + treatment (CPAP) significantly improves driving safety:
- Reduces crash rate by 50-70% in some studies
- Required for some drivers per medical examiner discretion

## Practical fatigue management techniques

For drivers:

### Before shift
- 7-9 hours of sleep
- Hydrate (not just caffeine)
- Light meal (not heavy)
- Mental prep (review route, expected challenges)

### During shift
- Strategic caffeine (early shift, not late)
- Hydrate
- Light snacks every 2-3 hours
- 15-min walks at stops
- Vary mental engagement (music, podcast, hands-free conversation)
- Bright cab lights at night
- Cool temperature (helps alertness)

### Recognize + respond to fatigue
- Pull over BEFORE microsleep
- Brief nap (20-30 min, not longer)
- Walk + stretch
- Cool water on face
- Don't try to push through

### Recovery
- 1-2 days off after long shifts
- Catch up on sleep (don't try in one night; spread over 2-3)
- Exercise + nutrition

## Common fatigue management mistakes

1. **Treating HOS as the goal.** Compliance ≠ safety.
2. **No fatigue education for drivers.** Drivers think "I'll know when I'm tired."
3. **Punishing fatigue self-reports.** Drivers stop reporting; just push through.
4. **Long-haul OTR without sleep apnea screening.** Major risk.
5. **Forcing night driving on day-shift drivers.** Circadian mismatch.
6. **Caffeine + amphetamines abuse.** Performance worse than no stimulant.
7. **Ignoring sleep environment quality.** Driver sleeps in noisy truck stop = poor sleep.
