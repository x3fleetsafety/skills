# Post-Crash Driver Care and Return-to-Work

A crash is a high-stress event for a driver, even with no injuries. How a carrier handles the next 30 days affects driver retention, safety culture, mental health, and legal exposure.

## Immediate (0–24 hours)

### Scene safety

The driver's first job: get themselves and others safe. Then call 911 if injuries, then dispatch.

### Carrier first response

1. **Confirm driver is safe** — check on driver before anything else
2. **Get scene details** — location, severity, injuries, other vehicles, weather
3. **Send safety/ops** — if local and serious, dispatch a carrier rep
4. **Notify insurance** — most policies require notice within 24 hours
5. **Photograph scene** — driver should take photos before vehicles moved (if safe to do so)
6. **Begin investigation** — per accident-investigation-playbook skill

### DOT-required post-accident drug/alcohol testing (49 CFR 382.303)

| Crash type | Test required? |
|---|---|
| Fatality (any) | YES — both alcohol within 8 hr; drug within 32 hr |
| Bodily injury treated away from scene + citation issued to driver | YES |
| Vehicle towed from scene + citation issued to driver | YES |
| Any other | NO |

If test required and not administered: driver cannot resume safety-sensitive duties until completed. Document why test was unavailable if not done.

### Communication to family

If the driver is hospitalized, the carrier (with driver's permission or via emergency contact) calls family. Don't let family find out from social media.

### Crash communications playbook

- Public: nothing public until insurance and legal cleared
- Internal: minimal detail in dispatch; no driver name on internal Slack
- Customer: notification per shipper SOP

## First 7 days

### Driver care

A driver who's been in a serious crash often experiences:
- Acute stress reaction (intrusive thoughts, sleep disruption, hypervigilance)
- Anxiety about returning to wheel
- Financial worry (lost pay)
- Concern about job status

Carrier response:
- **Confirm pay** — most carriers pay during initial off-time, communicate this immediately
- **EAP referral** — Employee Assistance Program offers crisis counseling within 24 hr
- **No interrogation early** — let the driver decompress before deep investigation
- **Check in daily** — supervisor brief call, no agenda, just "how are you doing"
- **Family notification of return plan** — keep family in the loop with driver's permission

### Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) screening

A serious crash — especially with fatality or significant injury — can trigger PTSD. ~15–25% of drivers in fatal-injury crashes develop PTSD symptoms.

Watch for:
- Avoidance of driving / specific routes
- Flashbacks
- Sleep disturbance
- Withdrawal from family/colleagues
- Substance use increase
- Emotional numbing

Refer to EAP. Don't push return-to-driving until mental health professional clears.

## First 30 days

### Investigation completion

- Police report (typically 3–14 days)
- Insurance adjuster determination
- Carrier preventability determination
- DataQ submission if needed
- Driver interview (after decompression period, typically 3–7 days post-crash)

### Return-to-work decision

| Status | Action |
|---|---|
| Driver medically cleared, no PTSD, preventable crash, minor | Coach + return to duty |
| Driver medically cleared, no PTSD, non-preventable | Return to duty, no discipline |
| Driver medically cleared, mild stress reaction | Phased return (light duty 1 week, then partner-driver 1 week, then solo) |
| Driver with PTSD symptoms | EAP + therapist + medical examiner re-evaluation before return |
| Driver with significant injury | Medical recovery first; reassess CDL fitness |
| Driver refusing to return | EAP + open conversation; not all drivers come back |

### Phased return options

| Phase | Duration | Activity |
|---|---|---|
| Office support | 1–2 weeks | Dispatch shadow, paperwork help, low-stress |
| Local short-haul | 1–2 weeks | Daylight, familiar routes, home each night |
| Regional / partner driving | 1–2 weeks | Co-driver for buddy support |
| Full duty | Ongoing | Return to assigned lane |

Not all drivers need all phases. Some return full-duty next week. Some never return to driving.

### Retraining

If the crash had a clear behavioral driver (following distance, speeding, distraction), retraining before return is appropriate:
- Smith System / DDC course (6–8 hr defensive driving)
- Driver-specific simulator session
- Ride-along with senior driver
- Documented coaching plan in DQF

## CDL fitness-for-duty re-evaluation

If injury, medication change, or mental-health concern, the medical examiner re-evaluates:
- Has CDL physical certification still valid?
- Any new medications conflict with DOT requirements?
- Vision / cognitive impact?
- Mental-health clearance if concerns present?

Driver cannot resume safety-sensitive duties until medical examiner clears. Document in DQF.

## Insurance claim coordination

| Phase | Driver involvement |
|---|---|
| Initial report | Driver provides statement to insurance |
| Discovery | Driver may give recorded statement |
| Litigation | Driver may be deposed |
| Settlement / trial | Driver may testify |

Carrier supports driver throughout. Many drivers find litigation more traumatic than the crash itself. Carrier-funded attorney support helps.

## Termination considerations

A crash alone doesn't justify termination. Factors:
- Preventability determination
- History of prior preventable crashes
- Severity / injury
- HOS / drug / alcohol findings
- Driver's response to coaching

If terminated, document carefully:
- Crash investigation findings
- Preventability determination
- Prior coaching / discipline
- Reasonable retraining offered
- DQF complete

Wrongful termination after a crash is a common driver suit. Documentation defends.

## Driver retention post-crash

A driver who feels supported through a crash often becomes the most loyal employee on the roster. A driver who feels abandoned leaves and tells everyone.

Specific moves that retain:
- Same-day check-in from owner / GM
- Pay continuity messaging
- EAP enrollment
- Phased return option
- Public peer support (cards, GoFundMe if family affected)
- 6-month follow-up "how are you really doing"
- Acknowledgment that crash was hard

## Common mistakes

- Treating the driver as adversary from minute 1 — destroys trust
- Pushing return-to-duty too fast — driver re-traumatizes on the road
- Skipping EAP referral — missing the leverage point
- Insurance-driven investigation without driver care — driver feels like asset, not person
- No documentation of phased return — exposure for "return-to-duty too soon" claims
- Family notified by media — preventable PR disaster
- Letting the crash define the driver — even good drivers crash; coaching > labeling
- Failing to update DQF with re-training documentation
